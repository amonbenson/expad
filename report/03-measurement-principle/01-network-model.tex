\section{Pedal Networks}
\label{sec:network-model}

The left of \cref{fig:principle} shows four ways to wire a potentiometer to a stereo plug, which carries tip, ring and sleeve, whereas a mono plug has only tip and sleeve. In the two common wirings at the top, the wiper sits on the ring or on the tip, and the sleeve is the grounded track end \cite{mission2014expression, nonlinearlabs2026pedals}. Less often, the wiper sits on the sleeve. Two-wire pedals, the rheostats, connect only tip and sleeve, with the wiper tied to one track end. A mono plug in a stereo jack additionally shorts the ring of the jack to its sleeve. For a wiper on the tip, this puts the two track halves in parallel, so a track of resistance $R$ at relative travel $x$ shows $R\,x(1-x)$, which peaks at $R/4$ at mid travel, so every value occurs twice. Potentiometer pedals therefore need a stereo cable.

Any passive resistive network between three terminals is externally equivalent to three resistors meeting in an inaccessible star point (\cref{fig:principle}, center) \cite{kennelly1899equivalence}, and only this equivalent can be recovered from the terminals \cite{borcea2012resistor}. The resistance from one contact to the star point is called an \emph{arm}: $r_\mathrm{T}$, $r_\mathrm{R}$ and $r_\mathrm{S}$ for tip, ring and sleeve. A potentiometer has one arm much smaller than the other two, the wiper, which is not exactly \qty{0}{\ohm} when its lead adds resistance. The ratio of the other two arms is its position. A rheostat has one isolated arm. The solver reports the relative arm resistances $q_i = r_i/R_\mathrm{tot}$ with respect to the total resistance $R_\mathrm{tot} = r_\mathrm{T} + r_\mathrm{R} + r_\mathrm{S}$.
