Existing solutions to the wiring mismatch described in \cref{ch:introduction} differ mainly in where the mismatch is resolved, and by whom. \emph{Switchable pedals} carry a selector that swaps the roles of tip and ring. Some Moog and M-Audio pedals offer such a selector, the M-Audio EX-P among them, so that one pedal works with the inputs of several manufacturers \cite{maudio2026exp, nonlinearlabs2026pedals}. On the instrument-side, \emph{configurable inputs} adapt to the pedal instead. The Nonlinear Labs C15, for example, provides presets for potentiometer pedals with the wiper on the tip or on the ring, for two-wire pedals and for switches, and its manual names the matching preset for a number of known pedal models \cite{nonlinearlabs2026pedals}. Both approaches cover the common wirings, but leave the decision to the musician, who has to know how the pedal is wired or find the right setting by trial and error.

Dedicated interfaces move the problem out of the instrument: they read the pedal themselves and pass its position on as MIDI, so that any device with a USB MIDI input can use it. \emph{Fixed-wiring interfaces} read the wiper of one assumed wiring with the ADC of a microcontroller and send the scaled value as a control change. The project by Gaspar, for example, converts the reading of a single pedal without any adjustment for its wiring \cite{gaspar2026diyexpression}, so a pedal wired differently meets the same mismatch as on an instrument input. \emph{Pedal-sensing interfaces} detect the connected pedal instead. According to their product pages, the Audiofront MIDI Expression recognizes the type and polarity of a pedal \cite{audiofront2026midiexpression}, and the DOREMiDi MPC-10 tells apart expression pedals of either polarity, sustain pedals and dual footswitches \cite{doremidi2026mpc10}. However, neither manufacturer documents how the detection works, which networks it distinguishes, or how it reacts to a pedal outside these categories. This project addresses the same task, but identifies the pedal by measuring its resistive network, so that both the classification and its limits are documented and can be tested.

The identification itself is more closely related to measurement instruments than to music equipment. The \emph{AVR transistor tester} determines the type and pinout of an unknown part connected to three test pins \cite{frejek2011avr}. Each test pin is connected to port pins of the microcontroller through a \qty{680}{\ohm} and a \qty{470}{\kilo\ohm} resistor. The firmware can thus pull every pin high or low through either resistor, or leave it open, while the ADC of the microcontroller reads the resulting voltages \cite{frejek2011avr, kuebbeler2023transistortester}. From a sequence of such measurements, the tester distinguishes resistors, capacitors, diodes and transistors and reports their values \cite{kuebbeler2023transistortester}. In its general form, recovering the resistors of a network from voltages and currents at its accessible terminals is known as the discrete inverse problem for resistor networks. Curtis and Morrow showed that the resistors of rectangular grid networks are uniquely determined by such boundary measurements \cite{curtis1990determining, curtis2000inverse}. A pedal, in contrast, has only three terminals, so its network reduces to the star equivalent introduced in \cref{sec:network-model}. This project applies the principle of the component tester to this case. Unlike the tester, which measures a part once on request, it identifies a pedal whenever one is plugged in and then follows its position with a single conversion per update instead of repeating the identification.
