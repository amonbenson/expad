\chapter{Introduction}
\label{ch:introduction}

\scaffold{
  Budget: about 280 words in three paragraphs, no figures, no results, no chapter roadmap (writing guide, section 3.3). The reader is electronics-literate but does not know what an expression pedal is.

  \textbf{Paragraph 1, field and structure.} Musicians who play with both hands change parameters such as volume or filter cutoff with their feet; an expression pedal is the continuous variant of these foot controllers \cite{mission2014expression}. Inside it is passive: a potentiometer, typically \qtyrange{5}{50}{\kilo\ohm}, connected to a \qty{6.35}{\milli\metre} TRS jack; the device supplies a voltage across the track and reads the wiper back \cite{mission2014expression, nonlinearlabs2026pedals}. Introduce tip, ring and sleeve; the term \emph{contact} denotes one of them.

  \textbf{Paragraph 2, the difficulty.} No standard defines the wiring \cite{mission2014expression}: the wiper sits on the tip (Boss/Roland) or on the ring (Yamaha) \cite{nonlinearlabs2026pedals}, and some pedals connect only tip and sleeve as a variable resistor. On a mismatched input a pedal reads reversed, with a limited range, or not at all; polarity switches mitigate this but have to be known and set \cite{maudio2026exp}.

  \textbf{Paragraph 3, \enquote{Concretely, \ldots}.} Concretely, this project builds an adapter with four jacks that identifies the network behind every jack electrically, follows the pedal and sends its position as a MIDI control change over USB \cite{benson2026proposal}, for potentiometer pedals of either wiring, sustain switches and two-wire rheostats. The identification applies the principle of automatic component testers, which drive the pins of an unknown part through known resistors and read their voltages with an analog-to-digital converter (ADC) \cite{frejek2011avr}; the solver runs as a host-tested library on the device \cite{benson2026expad}.
}
