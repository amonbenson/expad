\section{Timing}
\label{sec:results-timing}

For the identification time, the oscilloscope recorded the tip switch and the ring of one jack, triggered by the plug lifting the tip switch. Every change of the drive pattern shows on the ring, so the phases of the jack monitor can be read off. With a rail measurement less than \qty{30}{\second} old, a potentiometer pedal was followed 65 to \qty{95}{\milli\second} after insertion in five runs (\cref{fig:plug-in-fresh}): up to \qty{50}{\milli\second} until the next plug check and 44 to \qty{48}{\milli\second} of identification, or about \qty{60}{\milli\second} in the first run, where the plug was not fully seated and the first pair was repeated. Otherwise, the rails are measured first, which takes \qty{214}{\milli\second} and gives about \qty{300}{\milli\second} in total (\cref{fig:plug-in-refresh}). Unplugging was noticed within 2 to \qty{27}{\milli\second}: the next following reading no longer matches the pedal, so the monitor identifies the jack again before the next plug check. The solve finds no current, and the jack reported empty after 260 to \qty{391}{\milli\second} in three runs, again mostly because of the rail measurement (\cref{fig:unplug}). \Cref{tab:plug-in} lists all runs, and \cref{fig:plug-unplug} shows the two remaining captures.

An earlier capture of a power-up with the EV-30 plugged in contains a floating ring charging through the pedal. The fitted time constant was \qty{190.6}{\micro\second}, against $(R_\mathrm{tot} + R_\mathrm{f})\,C_\mathrm{f} = \qty{194}{\micro\second}$ from \cref{eq:settling}. After the minimum wait of \qty{1}{\milli\second}, \qty{3.2}{\milli\volt} remained, about $6\sigma_V$, which the averaging of the following conversion reduces further (\cref{sec:hardware}). A series with known resistors up to \qty{220}{\kilo\ohm}, which would test the settling model at its limit, was left out in favor of the other measurements.

The firmware logged 188 to 189 updates per second for one pedal on its ADC and about the expected 95 per jack for two pedals on one ADC. All four jacks were also run at once, with two expression pedals, a footswitch and an open cable, and each was identified and followed.

The end-to-end latency, from a pedal movement to the audible result, was not measured, since the share of the adapter is small against that of the host and is set almost entirely by the ADC readings. Within the adapter, a movement waits at most one reading interval, \qty{5.3}{\milli\second} for one pedal on its ADC or \qty{10.5}{\milli\second} for two pedals on one ADC, and half of that on average. The USB transfer adds at most the next frame of \qty{1}{\milli\second}. The host adds its own USB stack and the audio buffer of the music software, which collects samples before they are processed and played. At \qty{48}{\kilo\hertz}, a buffer of 256 samples alone takes \qty{5.3}{\milli\second}. With one pedal per ADC, the adapter adds at most \qty{6.3}{\milli\second}, about as much as this buffer, and stays below the bound of \qty{10}{\milli\second} from \cref{sec:firmware}. With two pedals on one ADC, its worst case of \qty{11.5}{\milli\second} exceeds the bound, while its average of about \qty{6}{\milli\second} stays below it.
